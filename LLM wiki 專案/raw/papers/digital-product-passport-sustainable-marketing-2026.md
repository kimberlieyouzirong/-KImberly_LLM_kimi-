## Abstract
The Digital Product Passport (DPP) is a regulatory and informational instrument, introduced within the European Union’s Green Deal and Circular Economy Action Plan, that provides a structured digital record of a product across its lifecycle, from raw material sourcing to end-of-life management. This paper examines the implications of the DPP for sustainable marketing, focusing on its capacity to enhance transparency, traceability, and credibility, and thereby to support more informed and responsible decisions by consumers and firms. Adopting a conceptual, theory-driven approach grounded in the economics of information and in signaling theory, the paper argues that sustainability properties are typically credence attributes that consumers cannot verify, and that the DPP can function as a credible signal only where its disclosures are independently verified and assured, in which case it converts otherwise unverifiable attributes into observable ones, reducing information asymmetry and constraining greenwashing. On this basis, the paper develops a set of propositions, each accompanied by research questions, that specify how the transparency, credibility, and engagement afforded by the DPP influence consumer trust, willingness to pay, brand differentiation, and participation in reuse, repair, and recycling. It also considers the principal challenges to DPP deployment, including data interoperability, privacy, and implementation cost. The paper contributes a structured research agenda for scholars, together with practical guidance for firms and policymakers seeking to align marketing strategy with the transition to a circular economy.

## 1. Introduction
The growing prominence of sustainability in consumption and production has placed a premium on the credibility of environmental claims. Sustainability properties cannot ordinarily be observed before or after purchase. The DPP emerges within the EU Green Deal and Circular Economy Action Plan, providing a structured digital identity documenting materials, origin, and full lifecycle from production to end-of-life management.
Prior literature on DPP focused on supply chain management (SCM) and technical architecture. This paper bridges the gap by examining DPP's role in sustainable marketing, consumer behavior, and firm competitive advantage.

## 2. Regulatory Context & Technological Infrastructure
- **EU Regulatory Framework**: Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (EU) 2024/1781 (ESPR); first binding regime in Batteries Regulation (EU) 2023/1542, phased into textiles and electronics.
- **Technologies**: Blockchain / Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT), IoT, decentralized identifiers for data integrity and real-time lifecycle tracking.

## 3. Theoretical Framework
1. **Economics of Information & Credence Attributes** (Akerlof 1978; Darby & Karni 1973; Nelson 1970):
   - Search vs. Experience vs. Credence attributes. Sustainability is a credence attribute (cannot be verified even after consumption).
   - Inability to distinguish genuine from deceptive claims leads to adverse selection and greenwashing.
2. **Signaling Theory** (Spence 1973; Connelly et al. 2011):
   - DPP acts as a costly, verifiable signal that produces a *separating equilibrium* separating genuine green firms from greenwashers.
3. **Theory of Reasoned Action** (Fishbein & Ajzen 1975):
   - Verified information -> positive consumer attitude -> purchase intention & willingness to pay (WTP) premium.
4. **Service-Dominant Logic** (Vargo & Lusch 2004):
   - Value co-creation across product lifecycle (maintenance, repair, take-back, recycling).
5. **Master Boundary Condition**: Data Verification and Independent Assurance. Technical immutability (blockchain) only prevents post-entry tampering; it does not verify truthfulness at entry. Independent auditing is essential.

## 4. Six Core Propositions (P1 - P6)
- **P1 (Transparency & Greenwashing Reduction)**: When independently verified, DPP transparency reduces perceived greenwashing and increases credibility of claims. Stronger for high-impact products, attenuated if information is overly complex.
- **P2 (Credibility & Willingness to Pay)**: Verified DPP credibility increases willingness to pay a price premium and purchase intentions. Moderated by prior scepticism and product category.
- **P3 (Empowerment & Circular Participation)**: Accessible DPP information empowers consumers to participate in use-phase and end-of-life circular behaviors (repair, reuse, recycling).
- **P4 (Value Co-Creation & Loyalty)**: Sustained DPP engagement supports value co-creation between firms and consumers, driving brand loyalty and advocacy.
- **P5 (Behavioral Data & Market Segmentation)**: DPP interaction generates granular behavioral data enabling precision segmentation and green attribute targeting (conditional on privacy safeguards & analytics capability).
- **P6 (Staged Competitive Advantage)**:
  - *Voluntary stage*: DPP adoption itself confers competitive differentiation through transparency leadership.
  - *Mandatory stage*: As DPP becomes compulsory, differentiation shifts to verified data quality and beyond-compliance performance exceeding regulatory minimums.

---

## 5. Research Agenda (Four Themes)
- **Theme A**: Signal credibility and verification conditions (blockchain immutability vs. point-of-entry accuracy).
- **Theme B**: Consumer response (perceived greenwashing, WTP premium, circular behaviors).
- **Theme C**: Boundary conditions (information usability, privacy concerns, category effects).
- **Theme D**: Voluntary-to-mandatory transition (competitive dynamics in EU vs. non-EU markets).
