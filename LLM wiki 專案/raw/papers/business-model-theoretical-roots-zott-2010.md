# THE BUSINESS MODEL: THEORETICAL ROOTS, RECENT DEVELOPMENTS, AND FUTURE RESEARCH

- **Authors**: Christoph Zott (IESE Business School), Raphael Amit (The Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania), Lorenzo Massa (IESE Business School)
- **Publication**: IESE Business School – University of Navarra, Working Paper WP-862, June 2010 (Rev. September 2010)
- **Classification**: Academic Literature Review Paper

---

## Abstract
The paper provides a broad and multifaceted review of the received literature on business models in which we examine the business model concept through multiple disciplinary and subject-matter lenses. The review reveals that scholars do not agree on what a business model is, and that the literature is developing largely in silos, according to the phenomena of interest to the respective researchers. However, we also found some emerging common ground among students of business models. Specifically:
1) the business model is emerging as a new unit of analysis;
2) business models emphasize a system-level, holistic approach towards explaining how firms do business;
3) organizational activities play an important role in the various conceptualizations of business models that have been proposed; and
4) business models seek to explain how value is created and captured.
These emerging themes could serve as important catalysts towards a more unified study of business models.

---

## Introduction & Background
In recent years, the business model has been the focus of substantial attention by both academics and practitioners. Since 1995 there have been 1,177 papers published in peer-reviewed academic journals in which the notion of a business model is addressed. While there has been an explosion in the number of papers published, researchers (and practitioners) have yet to develop a common and widely accepted language.

Key insights from the literature review:
- Despite the overall surge, scholars do not agree on what a business model is. 37% do not define the concept at all; only 44% explicitly define or conceptualize it.
- Literature is developing in three main silos:
  1) e-business and the use of IT in organizations;
  2) strategic issues (value creation, competitive advantage, firm performance);
  3) innovation and technology management.
- Common ground emerging:
  1) Business model as a new unit of analysis (spanning firm boundaries);
  2) Holistic perspective on how firms do business;
  3) Emphasis on organizational activities (activity system);
  4) Acknowledgment of value creation and value capture.

## Three Literature Streams

### 1. Business Models for e-Business (COO Lens)
- Primary driver: Mid-1990s Internet boom, decline in communication/computing costs.
- Two main streams:
  - Typologies and Taxonomies: Timmers (1998, 11 e-business models), Tapscott et al. (2000, 5 b-webs: Agora, Aggregation, Value Chain, Alliance, Distributive Network), Applegate (2001), Rappa (2001, Brokerage, Advertising, Merchant, Manufacturer, Affiliate, Community, Subscription, Utility), Weill & Vitale (2001, 8 atomic business models).
  - Components and Ontologies: Osterwalder (2004, Business Model Ontology / BMO), Gordijn & Akkermans (2001, e3-value ontology).
  - Revenue mechanisms: Free vs. Fee (Pauwels & Weiss 2008), advertising effectiveness and search keywords (Clemons 2009).

### 2. Business Models and Strategy (CEO Lens)
- Focus: Explaining value creation, firm performance, and competitive advantage.
- Locus of value creation: Spans firm boundaries in networked markets (Amit & Zott 2001).
- NICE Value Drivers (Amit & Zott 2001):
  - Novelty: Schumpeterian innovation in business model design.
  - Lock-In: Incentivizing repeat transactions, network effects, high switching costs.
  - Complementarities: Bundling products, services, or activities.
  - Efficiency: Reducing transaction and search costs.
- Difference between Business Model and Strategy:
  - Strategy focuses on competition, positioning, and value capture against rivals (Porter 1985, 1996).
  - Business model focuses on cooperation, partnerships, customer value proposition, and total value creation (Magretta 2002, Zott & Amit 2008).
  - Complements, not substitutes: Business model reflects realized strategy and execution architecture (Casadesus-Masanell & Ricart 2010, Richardson 2008).
- Contingency: Novelty-centered vs. Efficiency-centered business models interact with product market strategy (differentiation / cost leadership) and early market entry.

### 3. Business Models, Innovation, and Technology Management (CTO Lens)
- Unlocking value from technology: Technology per se has no inherent economic value; the business model is the heuristic logic connecting technical potential to market outcomes (Chesbrough & Rosenbloom 2002 Xerox study). A better business model beats a better technology (Chesbrough 2007a).
- Business Model Innovation (BMI): The business model as a source of innovation itself (open innovation, open business models, collaborative entrepreneurship).
- Incumbent Barriers to BMI:
  - Asset/Operational inertia (reconfiguration costs).
  - Cognitive barrier: "Dominant logic trap" (Prahalad & Bettis 1986, Chesbrough 2003) and "Identity trap" (Bouchikhi & Kimberly 2003).
- Overcoming Barriers:
  - Discovery-driven experimentation (McGrath 2010, Hayashi 2009).
  - Strategic agility (Doz & Kosonen 2010: strategic sensitivity, leadership unity, resource flexibility).
  - BMI Types (IBM / Giesen et al. 2007): Industry models, Revenue models, Enterprise models.

## Four Emerging Themes & Future Research
1. **New unit of analysis**: Spanning the firm and network levels.
2. **Systemic & holistic approach**: Content and process of business execution.
3. **Activity systems perspective**: The firm as an interconnected, boundary-spanning network of activities. Activity fit (Consistency, Mutual Reinforcement, Global Optimization - Porter 1996, Milgrom & Roberts 1990/1995).
4. **Dual focus on value creation & value capture**: Expanding from zero-sum capture to cooperative multi-stakeholder value generation.

Adjacent Literatures:
- New Organizational Forms (Romanelli 1991, Daft & Lewin 1993, Lewin & Volberda 1999).
- Ecosystems (Moore 1996, Iansiti & Levien 2004, Adner & Kapoor 2010).
- Activity Systems (Porter 1985/1996, Milgrom & Roberts 1990/1995, Porter & Siggelkow 2008).
- Value Chains and Value Networks (Porter 1985 vs Allee 2002, Parolini 1999, Normann & Ramirez 1993).
